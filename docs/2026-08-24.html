When AI designs a drug, who gets the credit?
MIT Technology Review digs into the increasingly thorny question of attribution when AI systems play a substantial role in drug discovery, using Insilico Medicine's generative AI platform as a case study. Insilico famously claimed its AI 'discovered' a drug candidate for pulmonary fibrosis — a claim that glosses over significant human involvement in validating, refining, and advancing the molecule through development. The credit question isn't just philosophical; it has real downstream consequences for patent law, academic publication norms, regulatory submissions, and how researchers and companies are evaluated and funded. If an AI system is listed as an inventor or co-discoverer, existing IP frameworks in most jurisdictions don't accommodate that cleanly. From a scientific integrity standpoint, overclaiming AI contribution risks creating misleading narratives that inflate investor expectations and obscure where human expertise actually matters. The flip side is that underclaiming AI contribution might discourage adoption of genuinely useful tools. There's also a reproducibility angle: if the AI's role isn't documented precisely, other researchers can't reliably replicate the discovery process. This is a question the industry needs to get ahead of before courts and regulators impose answers that don't fit the reality of how these systems actually work.

Is it legal to train AI models on copyrighted books? It's complicated
TechCrunch takes a thorough look at the legal murk surrounding AI training on copyrighted books, a question that remains genuinely unresolved despite years of litigation. The core issue is that virtually every major language model was trained on vast corpora that included copyrighted text scraped from the internet, often without the authors' knowledge or consent. Fair use doctrine in the US provides some cover, but its application to AI training at scale is untested at the Supreme Court level, and different jurisdictions have wildly different frameworks. Authors have a legitimate grievance: their work contributed to building systems that are now being used to generate competing content, often without compensation or attribution. On the other side, the labs argue that training is transformative — the model learns patterns and doesn't store or reproduce the original text directly, making it more like a human reading widely than like copying. Several cases are working through the courts simultaneously, and the outcomes could meaningfully reshape how AI models are trained going forward. The most likely near-term resolution involves licensing deals rather than a clean legal ruling — we're already seeing publishers negotiate data licensing agreements with AI companies. For developers building on these models, the legal risk currently sits with the model providers, not the downstream users, but that could change depending on how liability frameworks evolve.

The 'gig economy' model of algorithmic management is particularly exposed because the automated decisions have direct financial impact on people's livelihoods. Uber will almost certainly appeal, but even if the fine is reduced, the regulatory direction is clear. Thunder and fiber-optic cables used for seismic imaging of Earth's surface
Researchers have developed a technique that uses the seismic waves generated by thunder — 'thunderquakes' — detected through existing fiber-optic cable infrastructure to image subsurface geological features. This is clever opportunistic science: fiber-optic cables are already buried everywhere for telecommunications, and they're sensitive enough to detect the ground motion caused by nearby lightning strikes and thunder. Distributed acoustic sensing (DAS) technology can turn those cables into de facto seismic arrays without any additional hardware investment. The practical applications are substantial — subsurface imaging is used for everything from finding water aquifers to identifying geological hazards to mapping infrastructure, and it typically requires expensive dedicated equipment and often active seismic sources. Using atmospheric events as passive seismic sources, and telecommunications cables as the sensor network, radically reduces cost and expands where this kind of survey can be done. Urban environments, which are historically difficult for seismic surveys due to noise, might actually benefit from this approach since urban areas have dense cable networks and frequent thunderstorms. There's also a data quality angle worth noting: thunderquake-based surveys won't replace dedicated seismic surveys for high-precision applications, but for screening and reconnaissance they could be transformative. This is exactly the kind of infrastructure-reuse thinking that produces cheap, scalable scientific tools.

The honest caveat is that these systems typically work best in controlled laboratory conditions with training data specific to each individual, and generalizing to real-world use is substantially harder. Still, non-invasive BCI is advancing faster than most people realize, and papers like this mark real incremental progress toward practical clinical tools.
