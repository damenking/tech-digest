The Agentic Web Is Here
MIT Technology Review published a substantial piece arguing that the 'agentic web' — autonomous software agents navigating the internet on behalf of users — has quietly arrived, decades after Tim Berners-Lee first articulated the vision in his 2001 'Semantic Web' article in Scientific American. The framing is important: what Berners-Lee imagined required structured, machine-readable data that the web never fully adopted. What's actually happening now is messier but real — LLM-powered agents are navigating unstructured pages, filling forms, booking things, and taking actions without explicit human steps for each one. This isn't the Semantic Web, but it achieves some of the same ends through brute-force language understanding rather than structured ontologies. The practical implications are significant for developers: if agents are increasingly the 'users' of your software, your UX decisions, API design, and even your robots.txt policies become more consequential in new ways. OpenAI's simultaneous announcement this week that it will honor a shared robots.txt signal for AI training preferences is a related data point — the ecosystem is trying to build norms around machine access to content. DoorDash reported this week that outside AI agents have only resulted in hundreds of orders so far, suggesting mainstream agent adoption for e-commerce is still early. But the infrastructure is being laid rapidly, and the MIT piece provides a useful historical frame for understanding why this moment feels different from prior hype cycles. Worth reading if you're thinking about where agent-native product design is headed.

Mystery of 60-Second Power Grid Frequency Fluctuations Solved
Researchers at Forschungszentrum Jülich published findings in Nature Energy explaining the long-standing mystery of regular 60-second oscillations in the frequency of AC power grids — a phenomenon that grid operators had observed but couldn't fully explain, and that has taken on new significance as renewable energy sources with variable output are added to grids at scale. The culprit turns out to be a synchronization effect: as grid-connected generators and loads respond to frequency deviations, their control systems interact in ways that produce these regular oscillations rather than damping them out. The practical significance is real. Understanding the mechanism means grid engineers can potentially design control systems that actively suppress these oscillations rather than treating them as unavoidable noise. This is particularly relevant as AI data centers — which represent very large, sudden power draws — are being connected to grids in increasing numbers, and as the mix of renewable sources changes the dynamics of grid frequency control. The Nature Energy paper apparently went through multiple rejections before publication, which is itself a data point about how hard it can be to publish findings that challenge established engineering assumptions.
