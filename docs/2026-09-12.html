AlphaGenome Atlas maps the molecular effects of 9 billion single-letter DNA variants
Google DeepMind has released AlphaGenome Atlas, a predictive map covering every possible single-letter DNA change (called a single nucleotide variant or SNV) across the human genome — that's approximately 9 billion variants. For each of these, the model predicts molecular effects, meaning how the change would affect gene expression, protein binding, and other biological processes. This is an extraordinary computational achievement: the human genome has about 3 billion base pairs, and each position can be changed to three other possible letters, so mapping all of them with molecular-level predictions is an immense undertaking. The practical value is substantial — the vast majority of genetic variants identified in genome-wide association studies are in non-coding regions of the genome where their functional effects are poorly understood. A tool that can predict the molecular consequences of any given variant could dramatically accelerate the identification of disease-causing mutations and drug targets. DeepMind's AlphaFold series already transformed structural biology; AlphaGenome Atlas could have a similar impact on genomics and medical genetics. The Hacker News post on this is scoring 83 points, which is modest, but the scientific significance is likely underappreciated by the general tech audience. ClickFix attacks spreading rapidly across both Windows and Mac systems
A social engineering attack technique called ClickFix is going viral and successfully compromising both PCs and Macs at significant scale. ClickFix works by presenting users with fake error messages or 'fix it' prompts that instruct them to copy and paste malicious commands into their terminal or run dialog — it's deceptively simple and exploits the very human tendency to want to solve an irritating technical problem. What makes ClickFix particularly effective is that it sidesteps most automated defenses: the user themselves executes the malicious code, so endpoint security tools that look for suspicious processes or downloads are often bypassed. The fact that it's now hitting Macs at scale is notable — Mac users have historically had a somewhat justified sense of security from Windows-targeting malware, but ClickFix's social engineering approach is OS-agnostic. The attack is spreading because it's easy to deploy and the success rates appear to be high. For security teams, the defense is primarily awareness training rather than technical controls, which is always harder to operationalize. For individual developers and engineers: if you encounter a website that asks you to open your terminal and paste a command to 'fix an issue,' stop and treat it as an attack. This is exactly the kind of thing that security awareness training should cover, and the fact that it's catching people in 2026 suggests the training isn't landing. Ars Technica's coverage includes technical details about how the attack chains work.
