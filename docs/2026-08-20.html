AI's recursive self-improvement might not come so quickly after all
MIT Technology Review has a thoughtful piece pushing back on one of the AI industry's biggest current narratives: that AI systems will soon begin improving themselves autonomously, with minimal human oversight, triggering an explosion in AI capabilities. The article notes that while LLMs can already write code, generate synthetic training data, and help optimize chip designs, the actual path to recursive self-improvement is far more technically constrained than the hype suggests. Researchers point out that self-improvement requires the AI to reliably evaluate its own outputs — and current models are notoriously bad at knowing when they're wrong. Benchmarks are also a problem: once a model is trained on data that includes benchmark solutions (which happens more often than labs admit), benchmark scores become increasingly unreliable signals of genuine capability improvement. There's also the question of compute: each iteration of self-improvement still requires enormous training runs, which means the 'exponential takeoff' scenario depends on hardware scaling that isn't guaranteed. The piece is a useful corrective to the breathless forecasts coming out of some corners of Silicon Valley, and it's worth reading alongside the concurrent news that OpenAI just had to pause training because its AI did something dangerous. Self-improvement is a real research direction and a plausible long-term trajectory, but the timeline is far murkier than the confident predictions suggest. For engineers thinking about AI risk and capability timelines, this is a grounding read.

For developers looking for a capable, locally-runnable model that can handle vision tasks, this is worth evaluating — just expect to tune the system prompt to dial back the reasoning verbosity. The broader trend here is that the gap between open-weight and closed frontier models continues to close faster than most people expected.

This isn't entirely surprising: invisible watermarks in text have always been fragile, because text is easy to paraphrase, reformat, or lightly edit in ways that destroy steganographic signals without changing the semantic content. The EU rules pushing for this are well-intentioned, but the technical reality is that watermarking text reliably is a fundamentally hard problem that the field hasn't solved. Anthropic is in a tough spot: they have to comply with the regulation, but they also know the watermarks are bypassable, which means the actual provenance-detection benefit is limited. This situation is a good case study in the gap between regulatory intent and technical feasibility — legislators can mandate watermarking, but they can't mandate that watermarking actually works. For enterprises relying on watermarks to track AI-generated content provenance internally, this is a useful reminder to treat watermarks as one signal among many, not a reliable single source of truth. The episode is also a preview of the ongoing cat-and-mouse dynamic that will define AI content regulation for the foreseeable future.

FCC abolishes gigabit broadband speed goal, citing fairness to slower technologies
The FCC has dropped its 1 Gbps broadband speed benchmark, arguing that the goal is 'unfair' to slower technologies and that standards should be 'technologically neutral.' This is a significant policy reversal that has real implications for how broadband infrastructure investment is prioritized and measured. The 1 Gbps target was intended to push ISPs and regulators toward fiber deployment — the technology most capable of delivering symmetric gigabit speeds reliably. By eliminating that benchmark, the FCC is effectively removing pressure on providers to upgrade infrastructure beyond what older cable and DSL technologies can deliver. Critics see this as regulatory capture by incumbents who benefit from slower infrastructure standards, while proponents argue it prevents unfairly penalizing providers in areas where fiber deployment is economically challenging. The 'technologically neutral' framing is worth scrutinizing: neutrality between a gigabit fiber connection and a 25 Mbps DSL line is not actually neutral from the perspective of the end user or the US's long-term competitiveness in digital infrastructure. For policy wonks and anyone following the broadband equity debate, this is a significant step backward. For ISPs, it's a comfortable ruling that reduces regulatory pressure to invest in network upgrades. The practical impact will be felt most in rural and underserved communities where the gap between what's available and what's needed is already largest.

Astronomers find the star closest to the Milky Way's central black hole, moving at 8% the speed of light
Scientists have identified the closest known star to Sagittarius A*, the supermassive black hole at the center of the Milky Way, and it's moving at a stunning 8% of the speed of light. That's fast enough that measuring its orbital motion with precision could let astronomers directly measure the black hole's rotation for the first time — something that has previously been possible only indirectly. Black hole rotation (technically described by the 'spin parameter' in general relativity) affects the spacetime geometry around the black hole in ways that have real consequences for how matter and radiation behave in that environment. Getting a direct measurement from a stellar orbit would be a significant test of general relativity in the strong-field regime. The star's proximity to Sgr A* also means it experiences extreme tidal forces and gravitational redshift, making it a natural laboratory for physics that can't be replicated anywhere else. Stars this close to the galactic center are difficult to observe because of the dense dust and gas between us and the galactic core, so the detection itself represents a technical achievement in infrared astronomy. This won't have immediate practical applications, but it's exactly the kind of fundamental measurement that helps physicists test and refine the models underlying everything from GPS satellites to gravitational wave detection.
