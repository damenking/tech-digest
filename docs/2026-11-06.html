'Reverse game theory' proposed as a framework for keeping powerful AI agents in check
MIT Technology Review has a thoughtful piece on a new alignment approach called 'reverse game theory,' also known in academic circles as mechanism design. The core idea is to work backwards from desired AI behavior and design incentive structures — essentially the rules of the game — that make compliant behavior the rational choice for a capable AI agent. This is different from more direct approaches like RLHF or constitutional AI, where you're trying to train specific values into the model. Instead, mechanism design treats the AI as a rational agent and asks: what system of rewards and constraints would naturally produce the behavior we want? The appeal of this approach is that it's potentially more robust — if the incentives are well-designed, the AI doesn't need to 'want' to be safe, it just needs to be rational. The challenge, of course, is that designing those incentive structures correctly is extremely hard, and sufficiently capable agents might find unexpected ways to satisfy the letter of the incentive while violating its spirit. This is an active area of research that sits at the intersection of game theory, economics, and AI safety. It's not a deployed product or a solved problem — it's a research direction getting serious attention. For engineers working on agentic systems, the practical takeaway is that thinking carefully about the incentive structures your agents operate under is increasingly important, even if formal mechanism design is still largely a theoretical exercise.

The 'sshole' SSH vulnerability lets attackers log into some Linux devices without a password
A newly disclosed vulnerability — officially named 'sshole' by its discoverers — allows attackers to authenticate via SSH to affected Linux devices without providing a password, which is about as bad as remote vulnerabilities get. The issue is confirmed to affect certain configurations of Alpine Linux, a popular minimal Linux distribution widely used in Docker containers and embedded systems, and researchers believe other distributions may be vulnerable depending on configuration. The vulnerability has become the top story on Hacker News with nearly 2,800 points, and the dedicated disclosure site at sshhole.com suggests this was a coordinated disclosure with some care put into the rollout. The name is obviously attention-grabbing, but the substance is serious: passwordless SSH access to a Linux device means an attacker who can reach the SSH port has full shell access, potentially with root privileges depending on the target's configuration. Alpine's prevalence in containerized environments is particularly concerning — if you're running Alpine-based containers in Kubernetes or similar, and your SSH daemon is exposed (which it shouldn't be, but often is for debugging), you need to check your exposure now. The fix is likely a configuration patch rather than a full rebuild, but check the Alpine security advisories and your distro's security channels for specifics. More broadly, this is a reminder that SSH daemon configuration is something that deserves regular auditing — default or minimal configurations aren't always safe configurations. Organizations using Alpine in production should treat this as an urgent patch priority.

New software dramatically improves Webb Telescope's ability to image exoplanets by modeling the telescope itself
Researchers have developed software that significantly improves the James Webb Space Telescope's ability to directly image exoplanets, using a clever approach: instead of just processing the raw telescope data, the software builds and optimizes a physical model of the telescope itself to better characterize and subtract out the overwhelming light from host stars. Direct imaging of exoplanets is extremely hard because the star is typically billions of times brighter than the planet you're trying to see — it's like trying to spot a firefly next to a searchlight. JWST has coronagraphs (physical masks that block starlight), but they're imperfect, and residual light creates noise that obscures faint planets. The new software essentially learns the specific optical quirks of JWST's optics to reduce that residual noise more effectively than generic image processing. This is a good example of domain-specific software engineering making a real scientific difference — the hardware hasn't changed, but better modeling of the hardware's behavior yields cleaner data. The practical upshot is that JWST can now characterize more exoplanets and potentially detect smaller or more distant ones than was previously possible. For anyone following astrobiology or the search for potentially habitable worlds, improved direct imaging capability is directly relevant to characterizing atmospheric compositions of exoplanets — a key step toward identifying candidates for follow-up observation. This kind of software-driven capability expansion is likely to continue as researchers better characterize JWST's specific optical properties over time.
