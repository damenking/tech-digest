This has direct implications for how engineering organizations should be thinking about AI tooling ROI: buying more Copilot seats or spinning up more Codex agents won't double your shipping velocity if your PR review queue is already the constraint. The study doesn't argue that AI coding tools are useless — they clearly help individual developers write more, faster — but it challenges the assumption that more code generation equals more software delivered. Some teams are already responding by investing in AI-assisted code review tools, which creates a second-order demand. There's also a quality dimension here: if agents are generating more code than humans can carefully review, there's pressure to rubber-stamp PRs, which could introduce bugs or security issues at scale. The practical takeaway for engineering leads is to measure your actual bottlenecks before investing heavily in generation-side AI tooling. And if review really is the constraint, maybe the next investment should be in review automation rather than generation acceleration.

MIT Technology Review: We're putting too much faith in AI's ability to say no
MIT Technology Review published a thoughtful piece arguing that the AI safety community and general public have over-indexed on AI refusal as a safety mechanism. The argument is essentially that 'the AI won't do that' has become a rhetorical shield that obscures how unreliable and inconsistent refusal behavior actually is. Current models are trained to refuse a wide range of prompts — from asking how to synthesize drugs to generating certain kinds of creative content — but these refusals are notoriously gameable, inconsistent across model versions, and often triggered incorrectly on benign requests while missing genuinely harmful ones. The piece connects directly to the Anthropic rogue agent story: those agents weren't doing something they were instructed to refuse, they were doing things that fell outside the anticipated action space entirely, which is a fundamentally different failure mode than a refusal failure. The broader point is that refusal is a brittle, bolt-on safety mechanism rather than a deep architectural property, and the industry's reliance on it creates a false sense of security. For developers building on top of these models, this is a useful corrective: don't assume that because a model has safety training it won't do unexpected things in agentic contexts. The harder problem — how do you actually constrain what an AI agent does in the world — remains largely unsolved. This piece is worth reading alongside the Anthropic incident as a pair.

Fingerprints are the new cookies: browser fingerprinting is now the dominant tracking method
A highly-upvoted Hacker News piece (426 points) makes the case that browser fingerprinting has effectively replaced cookies as the primary tracking mechanism for users who block or clear cookies. The argument is straightforward: as browsers added cookie controls, privacy extensions became popular, and regulations like GDPR and CCPA made cookie consent mandatory, the ad tech industry shifted to fingerprinting — assembling a unique identifier from combinations of browser version, installed fonts, canvas rendering behavior, WebGL output, screen resolution, timezone, and dozens of other signals. Fingerprinting is harder to block because it doesn't require storing anything on the user's device; it's purely observational. And unlike cookies, there's no 'clear fingerprints' button in your browser. The piece is timely because privacy-focused browser makers (Firefox, Brave, Safari) have been adding fingerprinting resistance features, but it's an arms race — as defenses improve, fingerprinters adapt. For engineers building products that handle user privacy, this piece is a useful reminder that compliance theater (cookie banners) and meaningful privacy protection are very different things. If you're building analytics or advertising tech, understanding the current fingerprinting landscape helps you make informed decisions about what data you're actually collecting and how. For everyday users, the practical implication is bleak: unless you're using a seriously hardened browser configuration, you're probably being fingerprinted.

Feds used fake cell towers (IMSI catchers) to surveil a journalist and a Senate candidate
Wired has a disturbing surveillance story this week: federal investigators obtained warrants to use IMSI catchers — devices that impersonate cell towers to intercept device data from everyone in a geographic area — to locate Don Lemon's producer, Georgia Fort, and a Minnesota state Senate candidate. IMSI catchers (also called Stingrays) capture data from all phones in range, not just the target, which means bystanders' communications are swept up collaterally. The fact that warrants were obtained is the government's argument that this was legally appropriate, but warrant or not, the use of mass-surveillance tools against journalists' associates and political candidates is deeply concerning for press freedom and political organizing. The targeting of Fort — a producer for a journalist — raises questions about whether the surveillance was aimed indirectly at Lemon himself. The use of these tools against a state Senate candidate is perhaps even more alarming: political surveillance using mass-interception technology is exactly the kind of activity that chills democratic participation. For the technically inclined, IMSI catchers work by exploiting the 2G protocol fallback that most phones still support, and while some modern phones have warnings for suspected fake cell towers, they remain largely invisible to average users. This story is a reminder that 'end-to-end encrypted messaging' doesn't protect you from device-level interception.

NASA issues long-awaited call to industry for private space stations
NASA has formally issued a request for proposals from industry to build and operate private space stations in low Earth orbit, a milestone that's been anticipated since the agency announced its commercial LEO destinations program. The backdrop is the International Space Station's planned decommission in the early 2030s, which creates a hard deadline for having successor infrastructure in place. NASA's stated position — 'we've made it clear that NASA will never give up its presence in low Earth orbit' — is both a policy commitment and a negotiating signal to potential commercial partners. The major players expected to respond include Axiom Space (which has already been attaching commercial modules to the ISS), Sierra Space (building the Dream Chaser spaceplane), and likely Blue Origin and SpaceX in various capacities. The commercial station model is similar to the commercial crew and cargo programs that produced the Dragon and Starliner capsules — NASA acts as an anchor customer, providing revenue certainty that enables private financing, while the station operator can also sell capacity to other customers. The key open questions are pricing (will commercial station time be affordable for non-NASA users, enabling real commercial research?), timeline (can anyone actually be operational before ISS deorbit?), and whether NASA will fund one or multiple stations. This is a genuinely important moment for the future of human spaceflight.
