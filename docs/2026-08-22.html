The study doesn't argue for banning AI in education, but it does make a strong case that AI tools need to be designed with the learning process in mind, not just task completion.

Researchers use thunder and fiber-optic cables for seismic imaging of Earth's surface
Researchers have demonstrated a technique that uses thunderstorm-generated seismic waves, detected via existing fiber-optic cable networks, to image subsurface geological features. The approach is clever on multiple levels: thunder generates seismic waves ('thunderquakes') that propagate through the ground, and fiber-optic cables act as distributed sensors through a technique called Distributed Acoustic Sensing (DAS), which detects minute strain variations along the cable. This means researchers can use the vast existing infrastructure of buried fiber-optic cables — which crisscross cities and continents — as a passive seismic sensor array, triggered by naturally occurring thunderstorms rather than expensive controlled detonations. The applications are significant: subsurface imaging is used for everything from earthquake hazard assessment to urban infrastructure mapping to natural resource exploration. Traditional seismic surveys require either large arrays of dedicated sensors or controlled explosive sources, both of which are expensive and logistically complex. This approach could democratize subsurface sensing by leveraging infrastructure that's already in place. It's also a nice example of a scientific technique that becomes more powerful over time as fiber-optic infrastructure density increases — cities that are well-wired become better-mapped underground.
