Two competing Riemann Hypothesis proofs emerge — one AI-assisted, one purely human
In one of the most remarkable weeks in modern mathematics, two separate claimed proofs of the Riemann Hypothesis have surfaced nearly simultaneously, and the contrast between them couldn't be more interesting. Anthropic researcher Levent Alpöge claims to have proved the Riemann Hypothesis — and the Generalized Riemann Hypothesis — working with Claude, with the arguments also formally verified without assumptions. Separately, mathematician Alessandro Masullo has a proposed purely human proof of the Generalized Riemann Hypothesis that Stanford's Ravi Vakil says has been reviewed extensively by Brian Conrad and is 'looking good.' The Riemann Hypothesis is arguably the most famous unsolved problem in mathematics, with a $1 million Millennium Prize attached to it, and it has deep implications for the distribution of prime numbers and the foundations of number theory. The formal verification angle of the Alpöge/Claude proof is particularly notable — formalizing proofs in Lean is itself a significant technical undertaking, and doing so removes the kind of subtle human errors that have doomed previous claimed proofs. Hacker News is buzzing with a treemap visualization of the Lean proof and commentary on the staggering 17 million lines of Lean code involved, which is legitimately a lot and raises its own questions about verifiability. The BBC framing around the Anthropic researcher's claim is a bit loose — verification of either proof will take considerable time from the broader mathematical community. The timing of two independent claims so close together is either a remarkable coincidence or a sign that multiple researchers were converging on similar approaches. If either holds up, it would be the most significant pure mathematics result in decades and a defining moment for AI-assisted scientific discovery.

The 'time horizon plot' in question measures how long an AI agent can autonomously work on tasks without human intervention, and it's been widely cited (and widely misinterpreted) as a proxy for overall AI capability. METR's update is a rare piece of sober quantitative analysis in a field drowning in marketing claims. OpenAI's chief scientist has separately been talking about how to establish 'speed limits' on AI development, which requires agreeing on how to measure speed in the first place — and METR's work is directly relevant to that conversation. For engineers building on top of AI models, this is a useful calibration: trust your AI coding assistant, be more careful about anything that requires judgment calls. The report is worth reading in full for anyone who wants to cut through the hype.

Trump FCC preempts California's rules requiring AT&T to maintain home phone service
The FCC under Trump appointees has issued an order preventing California from enforcing its own rules that require AT&T to continue offering traditional home phone (landline) service in the state. This is a pretty significant federal preemption move — California had been using state public utility law to maintain what's known as the 'carrier of last resort' obligation, which requires phone companies to offer basic service even in unprofitable areas. AT&T has been lobbying hard to exit the landline business entirely, arguing that wireless and VoIP have made copper-line POTS (plain old telephone service) obsolete. The FCC's ruling says states can't use their own laws to impose requirements that the FCC itself has declined to impose federally — which is a broad interpretation of federal preemption that could affect other state-level telecom rules. This matters beyond just landlines: the same preemption logic could be applied to net neutrality, broadband availability mandates, or other areas where states have tried to fill federal regulatory gaps. For rural and elderly communities that still rely on landline service for reliability (landlines work during power outages; cell towers often don't), this is a concrete harm. It's also a preview of how the Trump FCC is likely to approach the balance between federal and state authority across the telecom sector.
