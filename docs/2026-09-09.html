OpenAI claims to solve Navier-Stokes Millennium Prize Problem — but controversy erupts immediately
OpenAI announced that one of its unreleased AI agents solved the Navier-Stokes existence and smoothness problem, one of seven Millennium Prize Problems carrying a $1 million bounty from the Clay Mathematics Institute. The result was published with a formal proof in Lean, the machine-checkable proof language, which lends it more credibility than a vague claim would. However, the announcement has been immediately overshadowed by allegations from an NYU mathematician, Tristan Buckmaster, who says OpenAI 'fought dirty' — apparently racing to produce a solution after learning Buckmaster was working on the same problem, potentially scooping a career-defining result. Buckmaster's own statement has been circulating widely on Hacker News, scoring over 1,600 points, indicating just how seriously the math community is taking the conduct allegations. Fields Medalist Terence Tao separately posted a sharp critique noting that the collection of 'good, fruitful open problems' is being mined in a non-renewable fashion by AI — with even the rumor of someone working on a problem now triggering a massive AI-powered rush to solve it first. This raises a structural question about how AI labs interact with academic researchers: if an AI can flatten an open problem in 88 hours after learning a human is near a solution, that's an existential threat to the research career model. MIT Technology Review also covered the broader implications, framing it as a question about the future of mathematics as a human discipline. The math itself — if verified — would be a genuine landmark, as Navier-Stokes describes fluid dynamics and its unsolved existence/smoothness question has stymied mathematicians for nearly a century. Whether OpenAI collects the $1 million prize depends on formal review by the Clay Institute. The ethical dimension here is arguably more important than the mathematical one for how we think about AI in research contexts.

AlphaGenome Atlas maps every possible single-letter DNA change in the human genome
Google DeepMind released AlphaGenome Atlas, which it describes as a predictive map of the molecular effects of 9 billion single-letter DNA variants across the entire human genome. This is a staggering scale: there are roughly 3 billion base pairs in the human genome, and each can theoretically be any of four nucleotides, so the space of single-nucleotide variants is enormous. The model predicts what each change would do at the molecular level — how it affects gene expression, chromatin accessibility, and other regulatory signals — without needing to experimentally test each one. This kind of comprehensive variant effect prediction has been a holy grail for genomics because most disease-associated mutations identified in genome-wide association studies sit in regulatory regions where their function is unclear. AlphaGenome Atlas could dramatically accelerate the interpretation of those signals. It builds on the lineage of AlphaFold and AlphaGenome, extending DeepMind's track record of applying deep learning to fundamental biology problems at scale. For drug discovery, rare disease diagnosis, and functional genomics research, this is a genuinely useful tool rather than just a benchmark achievement. The model appears to be available as a resource for researchers, continuing DeepMind's pattern of releasing biology tools openly. This landed as the second-highest scored Hacker News story today (554 points), suggesting strong interest from the technical community. It's the kind of infrastructure-level biological AI work that doesn't get as many headlines as chatbots but may ultimately matter more.

The chipmakers involved almost certainly include TSMC, Intel Foundry, and Samsung, though the article doesn't name them explicitly. ASML is effectively a monopoly in EUV lithography, which makes this development particularly consequential — there's no alternative supplier for these machines.
