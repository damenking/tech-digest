OpenAI claims to have solved the Navier–Stokes Millennium Prize Problem — but controversy follows fast
OpenAI announced that one of its (as-yet-unreleased) AI models produced a solution to the Navier–Stokes existence and smoothness problem, one of the seven Millennium Prize Problems that carry a $1 million bounty from the Clay Mathematics Institute. The Navier–Stokes equations describe fluid dynamics and have resisted rigorous proof for over 90 years — solving them is a genuinely big deal if it holds up. OpenAI says the process took 88 hours and includes a formal proof written in the Lean proof assistant, which is a good sign for verifiability. However, the announcement has been immediately mired in accusations that OpenAI may have 'stolen' the proof — essentially that AI systems were used to accelerate work that was already in progress by human researchers, potentially scooping them unfairly. Mathematician Terence Tao, one of the most respected voices in the field, posted on Mathstodon raising broader concerns: he's argued that the pool of 'good, fruitful open problems' is being mined unsustainably, and that even the rumor of AI working on a problem triggers a flood of AI-powered efforts that flatten it before human researchers can finish their work. MIT Technology Review noted that under normal circumstances this would be a massive feather in OpenAI's cap, but the ethical questions are now center stage. The formal Lean proof does give the mathematical community a concrete artifact to verify, which is more than many AI math claims offer. Whether the Clay Institute will award the prize to an AI system — or to OpenAI — is an entirely open question that nobody seems to have a clear answer to yet. This story sits at the intersection of AI capability, research ethics, and the future of mathematics as a discipline, and it's worth watching carefully as mathematicians dig into the proof.

DeepMind releases AlphaGenome Atlas — a predictive map of every possible single-letter DNA change in the human genome
Google DeepMind has released AlphaGenome Atlas, a tool that maps the predicted molecular effects of all 9 billion possible single-letter DNA variants (SNVs) across the human genome. To put that in context: the human genome has roughly 3 billion base pairs, and any one of those can be changed to any of three other nucleotides — AlphaGenome Atlas tries to predict what each such change would do at the molecular level. This is a genuinely massive undertaking and builds on the lineage of AlphaFold, which transformed structural biology by predicting protein structures. The implications for medicine are significant: most human genetic diseases and predispositions come down to specific variants, and understanding their molecular effects is the first step toward designing interventions. For researchers in genomics, this could compress years of experimental work into days of computational querying. The model presumably draws on large training sets of known variant effects from databases like ClinVar and gnomAD, and extends predictions to the vast space of variants that haven't been experimentally characterized. Like AlphaFold, the real test will be how well the predictions hold up experimentally — ML models for biology have a history of impressive benchmarks that are harder to reproduce in the wet lab. Still, even imperfect predictions at this scale are useful for prioritizing which variants to study experimentally. This is the kind of AI application — expanding the frontier of what's knowable in science — that tends to have long-lasting impact well beyond the initial announcement.

WeWorm: Researchers demonstrate first zero-click worm spreading through WeChat calls, built with AI in two days
A research group called Calif Research has published a demo of WeWorm, described as the first zero-click worm capable of spreading through WeChat calls on both iOS and Android. Zero-click exploits are the most dangerous class of mobile attack: the victim doesn't need to answer the call, tap anything, or interact with their phone in any way — the exploit executes silently just from receiving the call. The researchers say AI assisted in finding the underlying vulnerability and writing the first remote code execution exploit in approximately two days — a timeline that would have been dramatically longer using traditional methods. The worm component then self-propagates, meaning it can spread from device to device without ongoing attacker involvement. This is a stark demonstration of the 'AI-accelerated vulnerability discovery' concern that security researchers have been raising for a couple of years. For the broader industry, the two-day timeline is the number to sit with: finding and weaponizing a zero-click RCE in a major messaging app used to take teams of elite researchers weeks or months. The patch gap problem — the lag between a vulnerability being discovered and patches reaching end users — is already a serious issue, and AI-accelerated exploit development compresses that window further. WeChat has roughly 1.3 billion monthly active users, predominantly in China but with significant global diaspora populations. The responsible disclosure timeline and whether a patch is available isn't clear from the report. Simon Willison flagged this as significant, and given the attack surface (a ubiquitous messaging app, zero user interaction required, AI-built in two days), it's hard to overstate the implications for mobile security.

Samsung demos zHBM: stacking memory directly on AI accelerators — a potential step-change in memory bandwidth
Samsung has unveiled a prototype of zHBM (Zero-distance HBM), a technology that stacks high-bandwidth memory directly on top of AI accelerator chips rather than connecting them via an interposer on the same package. Current HBM implementations already provide dramatically higher memory bandwidth than GDDR, but they still involve physical separation between the memory stack and the compute die — zHBM eliminates that gap. The potential benefit is significant: memory bandwidth is increasingly the binding constraint in large AI model inference and training, as the compute-to-memory bandwidth ratio (arithmetic intensity) of modern transformer models often leaves GPU compute sitting idle waiting for data. Stacking memory directly on the accelerator could yield another step-change improvement in effective throughput for memory-bound workloads. The challenges are substantial — thermal management becomes significantly more complex when a power-hungry memory stack sits directly atop a high-TDP compute die, yields are harder to maintain, and repairability essentially goes to zero. This is still a prototype, not a product announcement, and the gap between a Samsung research demo and volume production can be measured in years. But it's a meaningful signal about where the memory architecture roadmap is heading, and for anyone thinking about AI infrastructure at a 3-5 year horizon, this is the kind of advance worth tracking.
