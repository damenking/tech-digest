MIT Study: Using AI Chatbots for News Makes People Worse at Spotting Misinformation
A study from Pattie Maes's group at the MIT Media Lab has a finding that should give pause to anyone who relies on AI chatbots to stay informed: participants who used AI to evaluate news headlines over four weeks became measurably worse at detecting misinformation. Specifically, people who initially evaluated paired headlines and images were 21% more accurate at telling truth from falsehood at the start of the study — but that accuracy degraded over time for those relying on AI summaries. The mechanism appears to be cognitive offloading: when the AI does the critical evaluation work, users stop doing it themselves, and the skill atrophies. This is distinct from the question of whether the AI itself produces accurate summaries — even if the AI is right most of the time, users who defer to it may become less capable of independent judgment. The finding has obvious implications for AI-powered news digest products (yes, including ones like this), and for the broader deployment of AI as an information intermediary. It's also consistent with what researchers have found about GPS and spatial navigation — tools that do the work for you can reduce your ability to do that work yourself. For engineers building AI-assisted reading or research tools, this is a design challenge: how do you preserve user agency and critical thinking while still providing value? The study doesn't argue AI news tools are net negative, but it does argue they need to be designed with this risk in mind. Maes's lab has a track record of rigorous work on human-computer interaction, so this result deserves to be taken seriously rather than dismissed.

AI Won't Replace Radiologists, But It Is Fundamentally Reshaping Their Work
Ars Technica has a thoughtful long-read on how AI is actually playing out in radiology — the specialty that became shorthand for 'AI will eliminate this profession' after Geoffrey Hinton's famous 2016 prediction that radiologists would be obsolete within five years. Ten years later, radiologists still exist and are still in demand, but their work is changing in ways that are worth understanding. AI systems are now routinely handling triage — flagging urgent cases, prioritizing worklists, and pre-screening for obvious findings — which means radiologists are spending more of their time on complex, ambiguous cases. The workflow change is real and significant: a radiologist today is more likely to be reviewing AI-flagged cases and providing clinical judgment than methodically working through a stack of scans. This has quality implications that cut both ways — AI can catch things humans miss, but radiologists may also be anchored by AI outputs, a phenomenon called automation bias. The piece notes that the 'AI replaces radiologist' framing was always too simple; the more accurate frame is 'AI replaces some tasks radiologists do.' For medical AI developers, the practical lesson is that the bottleneck isn't detection accuracy anymore — it's workflow integration, liability frameworks, and trust-building with clinicians. The story is a useful corrective to both the doom-and-gloom displacement narrative and the naive 'AI is just a tool' dismissal.

It's worth being appropriately cautious here: proposals that 'resolve' fundamental problems in theoretical physics circulate regularly and most don't hold up under scrutiny.
