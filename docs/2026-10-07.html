Hackers Obtain Counterfeit TLS Certificates for Google and Other Major Services
This is a serious security story that deserves attention. Hackers compromised three domain registries and used that access to obtain unauthorized TLS certificates for major services including Google. TLS certificates are the foundation of HTTPS trust on the web — your browser trusts a site because a certificate authority (CA) has vouched for it, and domain registries are part of the chain that makes that process work. Compromising domain registries to get unauthorized certs is a particularly nasty attack because it can enable man-in-the-middle attacks that are invisible to end users — your browser sees a valid certificate and shows the padlock. The scope of the compromise — three registries, affecting certificates for major services — suggests this was a sophisticated, targeted operation. The immediate implication is that any traffic to affected services could have been intercepted. Certificate Transparency (CT) logs are supposed to catch unauthorized cert issuance, which is how this was presumably detected. If you're an operator of services with sensitive users, this is the moment to check CT logs for your domains and verify your certificate inventory. The broader lesson is that the trust infrastructure of the internet has more single points of failure than most people realize.

Neutrino Physicist Francis Halzen Wins 2026 Nobel Prize in Physics
Francis Halzen of the University of Wisconsin-Madison has been awarded the 2026 Nobel Prize in Physics for his leading role in developing the IceCube Neutrino Observatory at the South Pole. IceCube is a remarkable instrument — it uses a cubic kilometer of Antarctic ice as a detector, with thousands of sensors embedded deep in the ice to catch the faint flashes of light produced when high-energy neutrinos interact with the ice. Neutrinos are incredibly difficult to detect because they barely interact with matter at all; the scale of IceCube was necessary precisely because the interaction probability is so low. The observatory has been instrumental in the emerging field of neutrino astronomy, providing evidence that certain astrophysical phenomena (like blazars and supernova remnants) are high-energy particle accelerators. Halzen has been associated with IceCube since its conceptual stages in the late 1980s, and the prize recognizes a career-spanning contribution rather than a single discovery. The award is well-regarded in the physics community as overdue. For anyone interested in astrophysics, IceCube's data continues to be scientifically productive, and the Nobel spotlight may increase public and funding interest in next-generation neutrino detectors.

The claim is that patients on these drugs show younger biological ages than expected based on their chronological age. This is significant if it holds up, but there are important caveats: these are claims from the drugmakers themselves, not independent peer-reviewed findings, and aging clock methodologies are still debated in the research community. GLP-1 drugs are already known to have benefits beyond weight loss — cardiovascular risk reduction, potential kidney protection, and signals in dementia research. Whether the aging effect is a direct mechanism of the drugs or a downstream consequence of weight loss and metabolic improvement is unclear. Still, the convergence of metabolic and aging research here is genuinely interesting and worth following as independent studies catch up.
