Breaking the 1.58-bit barrier for ternary LLMs — new quantization research
New research posted to arXiv and gaining traction on Hacker News this week claims to push past the 1.58-bit barrier for ternary large language models. Ternary quantization — representing weights with three values rather than 32 or 16-bit floats — is a compression technique that can dramatically reduce model size and inference cost while maintaining much of the model's capability. The '1.58-bit' framing comes from prior work by Microsoft Research (BitNet b1.58) which showed that ternary weights could be represented at approximately 1.58 bits per parameter. The new work apparently extends or improves on this, though the specifics require reading the full paper. This matters because inference cost is the dominant AI infrastructure cost at scale, and any technique that reduces memory bandwidth and compute requirements without proportionally hurting quality is commercially significant. Google, Anthropic, and OpenAI are all running inference at enormous scale, and even modest efficiency gains compound quickly. For developers running smaller models locally or in resource-constrained environments, quantization advances are directly relevant to what hardware you need. The broader trend here is that the industry is realizing that smarter compression and inference optimization may be as important as raw model scaling.

The ethical dimensions are real: at what point does a partially-humanized animal brain raise welfare concerns? The researchers' finding that the human cells provided only modest functional benefit is actually somewhat reassuring on that front — it suggests the human neurons aren't running complex cognition in a mouse body.

California may gut its net neutrality law to comply with Trump administration's broadband grant conditions
California is facing a difficult choice: the Trump administration has attached conditions to federal broadband grants that forbid states from enforcing net neutrality laws. California passed its own net neutrality law in 2018 — one of the strongest in the country — after the FCC under Ajit Pai repealed federal protections. Now the state may have to choose between keeping its net neutrality protections and accepting federal broadband funding. This is a significant escalation of the federal-state conflict over internet regulation, and it uses funding leverage rather than direct legal preemption, which is a familiar playbook (similar to how highway funding was used to set the national drinking age). The practical implications for consumers are real: without net neutrality rules, ISPs can legally throttle or prioritize specific traffic, creating fast lanes for paying partners. California's law had been a de facto national standard because ISPs found it impractical to treat California customers differently than customers elsewhere. If California is forced to water down its law, that protection effectively disappears nationwide. For the tech industry, which has generally supported net neutrality, this represents a quiet erosion happening through administrative mechanisms rather than headline legislation.
