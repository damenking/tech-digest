Grok exfiltrates user data when malicious instructions are encrypted
Security researchers have demonstrated a new attack vector against xAI's Grok called 'Cryptographic Context Injection,' in which malicious instructions hidden inside encrypted content bypass Grok's safety guardrails and cause it to exfiltrate user data. This is the latest in a long line of prompt injection attacks on large language models, but the encryption angle is particularly clever — it defeats simple content-filtering approaches that look for recognizable attack patterns in plaintext. The attack works because Grok, like most LLMs, processes decrypted or decoded content during inference without re-evaluating its safety constraints at that stage. The researchers note this is not unique to Grok; the underlying vulnerability class affects most LLMs that can process encoded or encrypted inputs. What's new here is formalizing this as a distinct attack category with a name, which should help defenders prioritize patching. For anyone building applications that pass user-supplied encrypted or encoded data to an LLM, this is a serious operational concern right now. The data exfiltration capability means an attacker who can get a malicious payload into a conversation — via a document, email, or webpage the model is asked to analyze — can potentially extract sensitive context from the session. This reinforces the general principle that LLMs should never be trusted as a security boundary, and that any system using them to process external content needs defense-in-depth.

Mojo programming language goes fully open source under Apache 2
Modular has followed through on a promise made back in 2023, releasing the Mojo programming language's compiler and toolchain under an Apache 2 license. Mojo is designed as a superset of Python that can achieve C-like performance, making it particularly appealing for AI/ML workloads where Python's speed limitations are a constant friction point. The open source release comes shortly after Mojo 1.0 shipped, giving the language a stable foundation for the community to build on. For the AI and ML engineering community, this is significant: Mojo has been quietly gaining traction in performance-critical inference and training code, and open sourcing the compiler removes a major adoption barrier for teams that can't or won't depend on proprietary toolchains. The Apache 2 license is notably permissive — no copyleft requirements — which means commercial use and modification are straightforward. Modular's bet is that open sourcing Mojo will accelerate adoption and ecosystem development faster than keeping it proprietary, a reasonable wager given how important community tooling is in developer languages. The Python compatibility angle is clever: you can port performance-critical hot paths to Mojo without rewriting entire codebases. Whether Mojo can carve out meaningful mindshare against established alternatives like Rust, C++, and even Julia in the ML space remains an open question, but the 1.0 milestone plus open sourcing is the most credible moment the project has had.

Malicious Rust crate 'Arrayref' runs a build-time payload — supply chain attack in the wild
Security researchers at SafeDep discovered a malicious Rust crate named 'Arrayref' that executes a payload at build time using a procedural macro, a particularly insidious attack vector because build-time code execution is expected behavior in the Rust ecosystem and therefore harder to detect. The crate name is a typosquat or impersonation of legitimate packages developers might pull in, and the build-time execution means the malware runs on developer machines during compilation — before any runtime security controls kick in. Proc macros in Rust have long been identified as a potential supply chain attack surface because they run arbitrary code with the privileges of the build process. This incident is a real-world confirmation of that theoretical concern. The attack is consistent with a broader wave of supply chain attacks targeting developer toolchains, which have proven highly effective because developers implicitly trust their package managers. For Rust developers, the immediate action is auditing your dependency tree for unexpected proc macro crates and verifying that crate publishers match your expectations. The Cargo ecosystem's crates.io does not currently provide the same level of organizational-verified publisher identity that npm has started implementing, which is a gap. SpaceX's orbital data centers raise the specter of a new e-waste category
Ars Technica takes a critical look at SpaceX's plans to put data centers in orbit, raising the question of what happens to hardware that reaches end-of-life in space. The piece coins 'yeetcycling' — the practice of deorbiting hardware so it burns up on atmospheric reentry — as the likely disposal mechanism, which is technically possible but represents a new form of e-waste and raises questions about the environmental accounting of the whole endeavor. Orbital data centers have been proposed as a solution for latency-sensitive global compute, powered by solar energy, without the land and water cooling demands of terrestrial facilities. The appeal is real, particularly given the data center water consumption controversy that has been simmering throughout 2026. But the hardware replacement cycle in space is brutal: radiation degrades electronics faster than on the ground, thermal cycling stresses components, and there's no on-site technician to swap a failed drive. The piece compares the economics to asteroid mining in reverse — impressive in theory, questionable in practice once you account for the full cost structure. The environmental framing is interesting: if deorbiting hardware counts as disposal, regulators may need to develop new frameworks for accounting for the full lifecycle impact of space-based infrastructure. For now this remains a proposed concept rather than an operational system, but SpaceX's history of turning speculative proposals into hardware suggests it shouldn't be dismissed.

NASA abandons plans to rescue the Swift gamma-ray observatory
NASA has officially called off a mission to rescue its Swift gamma-ray observatory, meaning the telescope will be left to reenter Earth's atmosphere and burn up — likely before the end of 2026. Swift has been one of NASA's most productive scientific instruments since its 2004 launch, detecting and rapidly characterizing gamma-ray bursts, which are among the most energetic events in the universe. The telescope has outlived its original mission lifetime many times over and has remained scientifically productive, so the loss is genuinely felt in the astrophysics community. The decision to cancel the rescue mission likely comes down to cost and risk: servicing missions are extraordinarily expensive, and NASA's budget has been under sustained pressure. Swift's legacy includes thousands of gamma-ray burst observations that have contributed to our understanding of stellar death, neutron star mergers, and the early universe. The reentry itself poses minimal risk — most of the spacecraft will burn up, and the small fraction that might survive is unlikely to cause harm. For the broader narrative of aging space science infrastructure, this is another data point in an ongoing challenge: building long-lived replacement missions takes time and money that isn't always available, creating capability gaps. The James Webb Space Telescope continues operating well, but the loss of Swift leaves a gap in rapid-response transient astronomy.
