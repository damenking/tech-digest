Let's Encrypt cutting certificate lifetimes to 64 days starting February 2027

This is a continuation of the industry trend toward shorter certificate lifetimes, driven by the argument that shorter-lived certificates reduce the window of exposure if a certificate is compromised and encourage better automation of certificate management. The move to 90 days was itself a significant shift when Let's Encrypt pioneered it, and 64 days is another step in the same direction. For organizations that have already automated their certificate renewal with tools like Certbot or cloud-native certificate managers, this change is essentially invisible — the automation just runs more frequently. For organizations that are still doing any manual certificate management, this is a forcing function to automate, which is frankly overdue. The practical implication is that any system that relies on long-lived certificates for operational convenience is going to need to adapt. Let's Encrypt issues a huge fraction of the TLS certificates on the internet, so this policy affects a very large number of deployments. The February 2027 timeline gives organizations about four months to prepare, which is reasonable but not generous if your certificate management is currently manual or semi-manual.

Scientists entangle a glass bead with light — a quantum mechanics milestone
Researchers have achieved quantum entanglement between a glass bead — a macroscopic object by quantum physics standards — and a beam of light. This is an experimental tour de force that the Ars Technica writeup describes as potentially enabling a new kind of quantum memory. Quantum entanglement has been demonstrated between photons, atoms, and increasingly larger collections of particles, but getting a macroscopic object like a glass bead to exhibit quantum behavior is genuinely difficult and requires extreme isolation from environmental noise. The significance here is partly fundamental — it pushes the boundary of what scale quantum effects can be observed at — and partly practical, since objects that can be entangled with light are candidates for quantum memory in future quantum networks. The research is at an early stage and the 'quantum memory' application is still speculative, but this is the kind of experimental result that tends to open up new research directions. For anyone interested in the long-term trajectory of quantum computing and quantum communication, this is a meaningful data point about how quickly the experimental frontier is moving.

Remembering Margaret Hamilton, whose code saved Apollo 11
Margaret Hamilton, the software engineer whose work on Apollo's guidance software is legendary in computer science history, has passed away. Hamilton led the team that wrote the software for the Apollo Guidance Computer, and her code's priority scheduling system famously prevented a mission-ending failure during the Apollo 11 lunar landing — when the computer began throwing alarms caused by radar switch settings, her priority-interrupt system ensured the critical landing guidance tasks kept running rather than letting the system crash. She also coined the term 'software engineering' at a time when the discipline was not yet taken seriously as an engineering field, and she went on to found two successful software companies after her NASA career. Hamilton's work is a foundational chapter in the history of software reliability and safety-critical systems, and her insistence that software deserved the same rigor as hardware engineering is an idea that the industry is still working to fully internalize. Her passing marks the end of a direct connection to the first generation of people who built software for life-or-death systems, and it's worth taking a moment to appreciate the scale of what that team accomplished with the computational resources available to them in the 1960s.
