Margaret Hamilton, computing pioneer and Apollo software lead, has died
Margaret Hamilton, one of the most important figures in the history of software engineering, has passed away. Hamilton led the team at MIT that developed the onboard flight software for NASA's Apollo missions, including the code that famously prevented an abort during the Apollo 11 lunar landing by prioritizing critical tasks when the computer was overloaded. She is widely credited with coining the term 'software engineering' itself, arguing at the time that software deserved the same rigor and professionalism as other engineering disciplines — a view that was not universally accepted in an era when programming was often seen as secondary to hardware. Her work on Apollo demonstrated that software could be life-critical, and her insistence on systematic testing, error recovery, and priority-driven scheduling laid groundwork that still influences how we build reliable systems today. The Hacker News community gave this story a score of 1,611 — the highest of any story in today's feed — which speaks to how deeply her work resonates with the engineering community. Hamilton received the Presidential Medal of Freedom in 2016 in recognition of her contributions. She had continued to be active in advocating for software engineering as a discipline and for recognizing the contributions of women in computing. Her passing is a genuine loss for the field, and it's worth taking a moment to read about her actual work beyond the famous photo of her standing next to the stacked Apollo source code printouts — the engineering decisions she made under pressure are remarkable.

2026 Nobel Prize in Chemistry goes to molecular handedness research with implications for life's origins
The 2026 Nobel Prize in Chemistry has been awarded to Henri Kagan and Kensō Soai for designing chemical reactions that produce only one of the two mirror-image forms of a molecule — a field called asymmetric synthesis. This is fundamental chemistry: most biologically active molecules are chiral (they have a 'handedness'), and life on Earth uses almost exclusively one handedness (left-handed amino acids, right-handed sugars). Understanding how to selectively produce one mirror image is critical for pharmaceutical synthesis — many drugs are chiral, and one mirror image works therapeutically while the other is inactive or even harmful. Soai's reaction in particular is remarkable because it's autocatalytic: the product of the reaction acts as its own catalyst, amplifying a tiny initial bias into a highly selective outcome. This has direct implications for theories about the origin of life, because it offers a plausible chemical mechanism for how life's initial chiral preference could have emerged and been amplified from a nearly symmetric starting point. The pharmaceutical industry implications are substantial — asymmetric synthesis is already widely used in drug manufacturing, and this recognition validates decades of work on making these reactions more efficient and selective. For anyone curious about the deeper story, Wired has a good companion piece explaining the science in accessible terms.
