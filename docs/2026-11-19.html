Human liver kept alive outside the body for a record seven weeks
Researchers in Sydney, led by surgeon and inventor Carlo Pulitano, claim to have kept a donated human liver alive on external life support for seven weeks — shattering previous records for ex vivo organ preservation. The details are pending formal publication, but MIT Technology Review reported the feat this morning. Current organ preservation technology typically keeps livers viable for hours to days, which severely constrains transplant logistics and means many donated organs are discarded because they can't reach a recipient in time. Seven weeks of preservation would be transformative: it would allow time for more careful recipient matching, enable transportation across much greater distances, and potentially allow for organ repair and conditioning before transplant. The technique involves connecting the organ to a perfusion system that keeps it metabolically active rather than simply cold-storing it. If the results hold up to peer review, this represents a genuine step-change in transplant medicine. The broader implications for biomedical engineering — including the speculative possibility of organ banking — are significant, though there's a long road between a research demonstration and clinical practice.
