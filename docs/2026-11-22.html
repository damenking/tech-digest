The Riemann Hypothesis appears to have been proven — twice, simultaneously
In what may be the most significant mathematical event in living memory, two independent proofs of the 167-year-old Riemann Hypothesis emerged within days of each other. Levent Alpöge, working with Anthropic's Claude, claims a proof that also resolves the Generalized and Extended Riemann Hypothesis. Separately, Alessandro Masullo — a human mathematician with no AI assistance — has been quietly working on the Generalized Riemann Hypothesis for months, with Stanford's Ravi Vakil reporting that Brian Conrad has been scrutinizing it closely and it's 'so far looking good.' The Riemann Hypothesis concerns the distribution of prime numbers and is one of the seven Millennium Prize Problems, carrying a $1 million prize. It underpins much of modern number theory and has implications for cryptography, physics, and pure mathematics. The AI-assisted proof has already been partially formalized in the Lean proof assistant, adding a layer of machine-verifiable confidence unusual for a result this new. The Hacker News discussion on Vakil's hurried announcement was the most upvoted story of the week by a wide margin (score: 2426), reflecting how significant the community considers this. Mathematicians are understandably cautious — extraordinary claims require extraordinary scrutiny — and full verification will take months. But the fact that two independent routes appear to converge on the same result is an encouraging signal. If confirmed, this would be the most consequential mathematical proof since Fermat's Last Theorem, and it would represent a genuine landmark for AI-assisted mathematical reasoning.

Researcher plants 2 million canaries into the AI supply chain, exposing a massive privacy disaster
A security researcher seeded 2 million unique, trackable 'canary' prompts — strings of text designed to be memorable and identifiable — into AI pipelines across the supply chain, then monitored whether those secrets leaked into other users' outputs. They did, at an alarming rate. The experiment proves definitively that data passed through many AI inference providers does not stay private in the way users assume. The implications are significant: enterprises sending proprietary documents, source code, or sensitive business data through third-party AI APIs may be inadvertently exposing that information to other customers. This is partly a caching problem, partly a context contamination issue, and partly a question of how inference providers handle multi-tenant workloads. OpenRouter separately disclosed data processing incidents on inference providers this same week, suggesting this is not an isolated finding. The canary methodology is clever because it doesn't require the researcher to identify specific leaked content — the unique identifiers are self-reporting. This kind of supply-chain privacy audit is exactly what the AI industry has needed but largely avoided doing. For engineers building production systems on top of LLM APIs, this should trigger an immediate review of what data is actually being sent to external providers. The researcher's framing of this as a 'leaky AI supply chain' is apt — the problem isn't any single vendor but the ecosystem of proxies, caches, and routing layers that have grown up around LLM inference.
