For web developers, the more interesting long-term question is whether Ladybird's independent implementation will surface undocumented behaviors in existing sites that only work because of Blink-specific quirks. The project's success depends on sustaining both engineering momentum and funding, which has been a challenge for independent browser projects historically.

Surveillance pricing: Companies are using your personal data to charge you more than others
A detailed piece examines how retailers and service providers are using personal data — browsing history, location, purchase history, inferred income — to present individualized prices to different users for the same goods or services. This practice, often called 'surveillance pricing' or 'personalized pricing,' has been growing quietly as data availability and algorithmic pricing systems have matured. The concern isn't just that you might pay more than average; it's that the pricing can embed and amplify existing biases — people in lower-income zip codes or from certain demographic groups may be systematically charged more. This happens even without explicit intent, simply because the training data reflects existing inequality. For engineers building pricing or recommendation systems, this is a reminder that optimization targets matter: maximizing per-customer revenue extraction is technically achievable, but the societal externalities are real. Regulators in the EU have been more aggressive about this than the U.S., but FTC attention to the practice has been increasing. The practical implication for consumers is bleak in the short term — using VPNs, clearing cookies, and shopping in private mode are partial mitigations but not solutions. Structural regulation is likely the only real fix, and that's moving slowly.
