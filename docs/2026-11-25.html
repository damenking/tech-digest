Matrix Multiplication Is Now Θ(n^2.25), Per OpenAI Math Preprint
OpenAI's math team dropped a preprint on November 24th claiming they've proven that matrix multiplication can be done in Θ(n^2.25) time — that is, the matrix multiplication exponent ω equals exactly 9/4. This would be a landmark result in theoretical computer science, one of the longest-standing open problems in the field. For context, the naive algorithm runs in O(n^3), Strassen's 1969 algorithm brought it to roughly n^2.81, and decades of subsequent work chipped it down incrementally to around n^2.37. Jumping all the way to n^2.25 is a massive leap. The paper is currently a preprint and hasn't been peer-reviewed, so the community will be scrutinizing it intensely over the coming weeks. If it holds up, the implications ripple well beyond pure math — matrix multiplication is the core operation underlying neural network training, graphics, scientific simulation, and much of modern computing. Even if the constant factors in the new algorithm are impractical, the theoretical bound matters enormously for future hardware and algorithm design. The Hacker News post for this is currently the top story with 766 points, suggesting the community is taking it seriously. It's worth watching closely — extraordinary claims require extraordinary verification, but the fact that it's coming from an OpenAI math team gives it at least some credibility as a serious attempt. Keep your eyes on the peer review.

What's Going On with the Riemann Hypothesis? An AI-Assisted Proof Looks Solid
There are now two parallel Riemann Hypothesis storylines running simultaneously, which is itself remarkable. On one track, Ars Technica reports that an AI-assisted proof of the Riemann Hypothesis appears to be holding up under scrutiny, though mathematicians are still working out what it actually tells us — proving it is one thing, but understanding the proof's implications for number theory and cryptography is another layer of work entirely. On the second track, Stanford mathematician Ravi Vakil (via Simon Willison's blog) revealed that Alessandro Masullo has a proposed purely human proof of the Generalized Riemann Hypothesis that Brian Conrad has been reviewing for months and is 'so far looking good.' The two proofs — one AI-assisted, one fully human — are circulating simultaneously, which creates an interesting meta-question about how mathematics will credit and validate results going forward. The Riemann Hypothesis, proposed in 1859, concerns the distribution of prime numbers and is one of the seven Millennium Prize Problems, carrying a $1 million reward. Its proof (or disproof) would have downstream effects on cryptography, since many encryption schemes rely on assumptions about prime number distribution. The AI-assisted version raises questions the math community is actively grappling with: does it count as a proof if the reasoning steps were generated by a model humans can't fully audit? This is genuinely uncharted philosophical and institutional territory for mathematics. Expect months of careful review before anyone pops champagne.
