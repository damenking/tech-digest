TypeSafe AI's Jev introduces 'Decision Models' — a fundamentally different shape of LLM
TypeSafe AI quietly unveiled Jev last week, and it's been generating serious discussion in AI circles this week. Jev is categorized as a 'System One model' by TypeSafe, though Simon Willison and researcher Maggie Appleton prefer the term 'decision models,' which is arguably more descriptive. Unlike a standard LLM that takes text in and produces text out, Jev accepts text inputs but outputs structured decisions rather than freeform text — think typed, validated outputs rather than prose that you then have to parse and validate yourself. This is a genuinely different design philosophy that addresses one of the most persistent pain points in production AI deployments: getting reliable, structured outputs that your downstream systems can actually consume without brittle prompt engineering. Simon Willison wrote a companion post, 'Jev in 25 Lines of Python,' and shipped an llm-typesafe plugin to integrate Jev with the LLM command-line tool, lowering the barrier for developers to experiment. A piece on Hacker News titled 'OpenAI is well positioned to fast-follow Jev' (score: 292) suggests the community already expects the big players to replicate this approach quickly. JevBench, a reproducible benchmark specifically for typed decision models, also appeared this week, which is encouraging — new model categories need new evaluation frameworks. The commercial AI labs have long offered structured output modes (like OpenAI's JSON mode or function calling), but Jev appears to make this the primary interface rather than a tacked-on feature. Whether this catches on depends on how much the developer community values type safety versus flexibility, but the interest level suggests it's touching a real nerve. Keep an eye on this one.

Data centers switching from copper to optical interconnects to cut power and boost capacity
A BBC Technology report examines the accelerating shift in data center interconnects from traditional copper wiring to optical (light-based) transmission, driven primarily by the power and bandwidth demands of AI workloads. The core problem with copper at data center scale is that it generates significant heat and has bandwidth limitations that become more acute as rack densities increase — exactly the trend that AI accelerator deployments are driving. Optical interconnects transmit data using light rather than electrical signals, which dramatically reduces resistive losses and enables much higher bandwidth over longer distances within a facility. The switch is not cheap or simple: it requires replacing significant amounts of physical infrastructure and updating the electronics that interface between optical and electrical domains. But the power savings can be substantial — power that doesn't go into heating copper can go into actual compute. This development is closely related to the IEEE Spectrum coverage this week of liquid cooling for AI servers, where rack power densities are now exceeding 250kW in high-end AI deployments. Together, optical interconnects and advanced cooling represent the infrastructure layer that has to scale alongside the AI compute it supports. For anyone building or speccing large-scale AI infrastructure, both of these developments are worth understanding as they shift from emerging technology to operational standard.
