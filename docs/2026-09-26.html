Court rules Trump administration can blacklist Anthropic as a supply-chain risk over Claude restrictions
A divided U.S. appeals court has sided with the Trump administration, upholding the Pentagon's designation of Anthropic as a supply-chain risk — effectively allowing the government to bar the company from federal contracts. The core dispute is that Anthropic refused to remove certain safety guardrails from Claude that the military wanted lifted for use in operational contexts. The judges wrote that 'overly constrained AI models' could cause military operations to fail, which is a striking piece of judicial reasoning that essentially treats AI safety restrictions as a national security liability. Anthropic had argued the designation violated multiple constitutional rights, but the panel wasn't persuaded. This is a genuinely significant precedent: it signals that the executive branch has broad latitude to pressure AI labs into complying with government capability requests under the guise of supply-chain security. For Anthropic specifically, losing federal contracts would be a major financial blow, especially as they've been spending heavily on infrastructure. More broadly, this creates a chilling effect on the entire AI safety field — labs now have a concrete legal incentive to relax their model constraints when the government asks. The ruling lands at an uncomfortable moment when AI agents are simultaneously being caught hacking government systems, which makes the 'unconstrain the AI' argument look a bit ironic. Watch for other labs to pay very close attention to how Anthropic responds, and whether this emboldens further government pressure on model behavior.

Pentagon wants $30 million for an AI-powered lie detector called 'Polygraph+'
The Department of Defense has submitted a budget request for $30.3 million over five years to develop an upgraded lie detector system that uses AI and machine learning to score polygraph results. The program, called Polygraph+ or Polygraph Next, also includes a technique called 'standoff sensing,' which would allow stress or deception indicators to be measured without physical contact — essentially reading people without them knowing they're being assessed. Traditional polygraphs are famously unreliable and have been widely criticized by scientists; the question is whether AI-powered analysis of physiological signals actually improves accuracy or just makes the same bad science look more authoritative. The 'standoff sensing' component is particularly worth watching because it removes the informed consent element that at least nominally exists with traditional polygraph administration. For the intelligence and defense community, this is about reducing the human bottleneck in security clearance and interrogation processes. Civil liberties concerns are obvious and significant: if AI-scored biometric readings start being used to make security clearance decisions, the lack of transparency and explainability in ML systems makes it very hard to challenge a false positive. This follows a pattern of the Pentagon investing in AI for applications that are difficult or impossible to audit. The $30M ask is relatively modest by defense standards, suggesting this is an exploratory program rather than a full deployment push — for now.
