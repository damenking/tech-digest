Physicists trap a single antimatter particle for a month — longest yet
Physicists have successfully trapped a single antimatter particle for a full month, setting a new record for antimatter confinement duration. This is a significant experimental achievement: antimatter annihilates instantly on contact with regular matter, so holding any of it long enough to study it requires extraordinarily precise magnetic trap design and near-perfect vacuum conditions. The longer you can trap an antimatter particle, the more precise your measurements of its properties can be — and those measurements are what let physicists test whether matter and antimatter behave symmetrically under fundamental physical laws. If they don't behave symmetrically (a property called CP violation), that asymmetry could help explain why the universe has so much more matter than antimatter, which is one of the deepest open questions in physics. A month of confinement opens up experimental windows that were simply unavailable with shorter trapping times, including some spectroscopic measurements that require extended observation periods. This is the kind of patient, infrastructure-heavy experimental physics that doesn't get as much attention as theoretical breakthroughs but is how the field actually makes progress. The facility doing this work is almost certainly CERN's ALPHA experiment, which has been steadily pushing confinement records for years.
