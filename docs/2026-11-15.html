The scandal has multiple layers: Nvidia's security chief had already quietly departed in August, and a former intelligence officer quoted by TechCrunch notes that the kind of relationship-based intelligence leak involved here is exactly what most corporate security teams are poorly equipped to detect. The fallout is hitting the AI startup ecosystem hard — Nvidia under Huang had poured tens of billions of dollars into AI startup investments, and VCs and founders are now bracing for smaller checks, reduced valuations, and a funding vacuum that won't be easy to fill. Over 115,000 people, including Elon Musk and other prominent tech executives, signed a petition calling on Nvidia's board to reinstate Huang, which says something about how central he was to the industry's self-image. Asian governments are scrambling to respond: some are rushing to confirm chip supply commitments, others are monitoring impacts on domestic AI and semiconductor projects, and Singapore — a major chip hub — is facing uncomfortable questions about its role as a potential transit point for illicit chip trade. The BBC reports that Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick is facing growing political pressure, with Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent revealing that multiple government agencies had favored listing Megaspeed (the company at the center of the chip smuggling allegations) on the entity list for months before the scandal broke. This is not just a corporate governance story — it cuts directly into the US-China chip war, the credibility of export controls, and the question of whether Silicon Valley's coziness with power creates national security blind spots. MIT Technology Review had already been covering Nvidia's behind-the-scenes acknowledgment that chip tracking under the Chip Security Act was technically feasible, even as the industry publicly called it infeasible — that context makes the smuggling allegations sting more. Whoever takes the helm at Nvidia will inherit a company that is still the undisputed king of AI compute, but faces an unusually fraught political and regulatory environment. This is the biggest leadership story in tech in years, and the aftershocks will keep coming.

Foster, a Democrat on the Committee on Science, Space, and Technology, shared the details of his conversation with Nvidia in an interview with MIT Tech Review. The gap between private acknowledgment and public lobbying is striking — it suggests the industry knew these safeguards were achievable but chose to fight them anyway, presumably to avoid the operational overhead. Given that the Huang scandal centers on chips allegedly reaching China through third parties, the timing of this revelation is particularly damning. If Nvidia had supported chip tracking legislation rather than lobbying against it through trade groups, it's at least arguable that the smuggling pipeline could have been detected earlier. This story is a good reminder that industry objections to regulation are not always made in good faith — 'technically infeasible' is a phrase that sometimes means 'we'd prefer not to.' The Chip Security Act now has fresh political wind behind it, and expect this MIT Tech Review scoop to be cited extensively in upcoming Congressional hearings.

DeepMind's AlphaProof Code writes 3 million lines of Lean proofs to verify Signal's encryption
Google DeepMind announced AlphaProof Code, an agentic system that wrote three million lines of formal proofs in the Lean theorem prover to verify the correctness of Signal's core encryption implementation. This is a genuinely impressive result that sits at the intersection of AI and formal verification — two fields that have historically had very little to do with each other. Formal verification of cryptographic protocols is notoriously difficult and time-consuming; having an AI system generate the proof artifacts at scale could dramatically lower the cost of formally verifying security-critical software. Signal's encryption being the target is a smart choice for a showcase — it's widely trusted, widely deployed, and the stakes of getting it wrong are real. The system builds on the original AlphaProof work, which was focused on mathematical olympiad problems, and this represents a significant extension into software verification. For the security engineering community, this is worth taking seriously: if AlphaProof Code can be applied to other widely used cryptographic libraries, it could raise the floor for verified security in ways that manual audits simply can't match at scale. The caveat is that formal verification proves correctness relative to a specification — if the spec is wrong, the proof doesn't help. But within that limitation, this is a meaningful capability advance.
