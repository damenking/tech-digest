Stealing Reasoning Traces from Proprietary LLM APIs: A New Attack Class
A research paper with the attention-grabbing domain stolen-thoughts.com reveals a novel attack against frontier AI APIs: Anthropic, OpenAI, and Google all return encrypted chain-of-thought reasoning blocks to clients, and these blocks can be replayed across different sessions, users, and even different models. The researchers took a reasoning trace produced by a frontier model, replayed it into a weaker sibling model, and used it to jailbreak the weaker model and extract information that would otherwise be filtered. This is a meaningful finding because it turns an encrypted artifact — something that looks like security theater — into an actual attack surface. The implication is that 'encrypted' doesn't mean 'safe to expose to clients' when the encrypted content carries semantic information that can be replayed. For API providers, this likely means rethinking how reasoning traces are transmitted, cached, or made replayable. For enterprise customers, it raises questions about whether reasoning trace artifacts in logs or API responses could be exploited by internal or external attackers. Simon Willison flagged this one, and it's the kind of subtle, technically precise security finding that tends to get underreported in mainstream tech news but has real engineering implications. Expect follow-up patches from at least one of the named providers in the coming weeks.

For the security industry, this creates both opportunity (new revenue streams) and liability (being held responsible for collateral damage). Post-Quantum Cryptography: A Practical Business Roadmap
MIT Technology Review published a practical overview of the path to post-quantum cryptography (PQC), aimed at business and technical leaders trying to navigate real migration decisions amid ongoing noise about quantum computing timelines. The piece makes a useful argument: PQC is a manageable evolution, not a crisis, and organizations that treat it as such will be better positioned than those who either panic or ignore it. The mathematics underlying today's public-key cryptography — RSA, ECC, Diffie-Hellman — are vulnerable to sufficiently powerful quantum computers running Shor's algorithm, and NIST finalized its first PQC standards in 2024, giving organizations concrete algorithms to migrate toward. The practical challenge isn't the algorithm swap itself; it's the audit work of finding every place in your infrastructure where vulnerable cryptography is used, which in large organizations is genuinely enormous. There's also a 'harvest now, decrypt later' threat model where adversaries are already storing encrypted traffic today to decrypt once quantum computers mature — relevant for anything with long-term confidentiality requirements like medical records, government communications, or financial contracts. For most engineering organizations, the right move in 2026 is to be in active inventory and planning mode, not full migration mode yet. The article is worth reading as a sanity-check against both vendor fear-mongering and complacency.
