MIT Tech Review: LLMs Don't Actually Reason — A Timely Reminder
MIT Technology Review published a pointed essay arguing that large language models don't reason — they pattern-match in ways that can look like reasoning but fundamentally aren't. The author draws on firsthand experience watching AlphaGo's famous Move 37 in 2016, using it to illustrate how AI systems can produce outputs that appear deeply strategic without any underlying model of the world. This is a recurring debate that tends to flare up whenever a new frontier model impresses people on benchmarks — and with GPT-6 Astra and Gemini 4 Argon both landing this week, the timing is pointed. The essay's core argument is that LLMs are doing sophisticated statistical interpolation over training data, not building causal models or doing genuine logical deduction. The practical implication for engineers is real: systems that appear to reason correctly on seen distributions will fail in surprising ways on novel distributions, and you can't always predict when that will happen. This matters a lot for high-stakes deployments — legal, medical, financial — where people are increasingly tempted to lean on LLM outputs as if they were reliable reasoners. The counter-argument, which the essay doesn't fully dismiss, is that the distinction between 'real reasoning' and 'very good pattern matching' may matter less in practice than it does philosophically. Still, the essay is a useful corrective to the hype cycle, especially given the number of enterprise AI deployments being announced this week. Worth reading alongside the Bank of England governor's warning that the AI investment boom could trigger market shocks — both are saying, in different registers, that the gap between perceived and actual AI capabilities has real consequences.

OpenBSD Developers Reject Uutils Coreutils Port Over Licensing and Compatibility Concerns
OpenBSD developers have rejected a port of uutils coreutils — the Rust-based reimplementation of the GNU core utilities — citing licensing and compatibility concerns. Uutils aims to be a drop-in replacement for GNU coreutils written in Rust, offering memory safety benefits and, for some projects, a more permissive license than GPL. OpenBSD's rejection is notable because the project is generally very deliberate about what enters its base system, and the reasons given — licensing and compatibility — point to real tensions in the open source ecosystem. OpenBSD uses its own BSD-licensed utilities and has historically been wary of GPL dependencies in its base; uutils is MIT/Apache licensed, which should be compatible, so the licensing concern may relate to specific components or dependency chains. The compatibility concern is probably more significant: OpenBSD's userland has specific behavioral expectations, and a reimplementation that passes most tests but diverges on edge cases could introduce subtle bugs. This is a recurring challenge for Rust rewrites of established C tools: correctness at the edge cases matters enormously for a base operating system, and 'mostly compatible' isn't good enough. For the Rust ecosystem, this isn't a devastating setback — uutils is widely used elsewhere — but it's a useful calibration on the limits of 'rewrite it in Rust' as a strategy for getting into conservative, security-focused OS projects.
