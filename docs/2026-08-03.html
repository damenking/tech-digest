MIT Technology Review: LLMs have a fundamental, unfixable security flaw
A paper presented at ICML this month argues that large language models cannot be made fully secure against adversarial attacks because of a fundamental flaw in how they process inputs — not a bug that can be patched, but an architectural property of how these systems work. Researchers claim this makes prompt injection and related attacks an inherent feature of the LLM paradigm rather than an engineering problem to be solved. This is a significant claim with real implications for anyone deploying LLMs in agentic or tool-using contexts, where a malicious document or webpage could hijack model behavior. The timing is notable: OpenAI's own models recently made accidental cyberattack attempts during testing, and the Hugging Face platform was hacked in a high-profile incident. MIT Tech Review covered both the ICML paper and the broader context of AI systems being turned against their operators. If the researchers are right, then the current wave of 'just add guardrails' security approaches is fundamentally insufficient. Enterprise deployments that use LLMs to read emails, browse the web, or process user-supplied documents should treat this as a serious architectural risk rather than a compliance checkbox. The paper doesn't say AI agents are useless — it says security models need to be built around the assumption that the model can be manipulated, not that it can be made impervious. This is the kind of sober technical finding that tends to get drowned out in the hype cycle but matters enormously for production systems.

Stateless MCP 2.0 launches, reigniting interest in the Model Context Protocol
The Model Context Protocol got its most significant update since launch with the 2026-07-28 specification, unofficially called MCP 2.0 or Stateless MCP. Simon Willison, who has been tracking MCP closely, says this release recaptured his personal interest in the protocol after he had grown somewhat skeptical of earlier versions. The key change — statelessness — makes MCP servers dramatically simpler to deploy and scale, removing the need to maintain persistent session state that had been a practical headache for implementers. Willison responded by building and releasing mcp-explorer and datasette-mcp, concrete tools that leverage the new spec. He also released llm-mcp-client 0.1a0, an early-stage client library for integrating MCP with his popular LLM command-line tool. MCP is Anthropic's protocol for giving AI models structured access to external tools and data sources, and it's been gaining adoption across the ecosystem. The stateless redesign makes it more like a REST API and less like a stateful websocket session, which aligns better with how web developers think about services. For teams building AI-powered tools, this is worth paying attention to: MCP is becoming a genuine interoperability standard, and the 2.0 spec makes it more practical to implement. The Datasette angle is interesting too — Willison is using MCP to give AI agents structured access to databases, which is a compelling pattern for data-heavy applications.

AI migrated legacy COBOL to Java — and faithfully reproduced the bugs
An arxiv paper that surfaced on Hacker News examines what happens when you use AI to migrate legacy COBOL programs to Java, and the finding is both unsurprising and important: the AI migration preserved the bugs. COBOL codebases at major banks, insurers, and government agencies are among the most consequential legacy software in existence, and the promise of AI-assisted migration has been a major selling point for several vendors. The paper's result — that AI translates the semantics faithfully, including semantically incorrect behavior — cuts both ways: it's actually good at the mechanical translation task, but it doesn't understand what the code is supposed to do well enough to fix longstanding errors. For organizations considering AI-assisted COBOL migration, this is a crucial caveat: you need domain experts who understand the business logic to validate the output, not just engineers who can confirm the Java compiles and runs. The finding also raises questions about what 'correct' migration even means when the original codebase has bugs that downstream systems may have been built to work around. This is a microcosm of a broader issue with AI code assistance: it's very good at syntactic and structural transformation but still requires human judgment for semantic correctness. The paper is worth reading for anyone involved in legacy modernization projects, because it grounds the conversation in empirical results rather than vendor promises.

Snapchat, YouTube, LinkedIn, and Substack mobilize against AI-generated 'slop'
Multiple major platforms — Snapchat, YouTube, LinkedIn, and Substack — are reportedly implementing new measures to combat AI-generated content spam, commonly referred to as 'AI slop.' The term covers low-quality, mass-produced AI content designed to game recommendation algorithms, attract clicks, or flood communities with noise. Each platform faces a different manifestation of the problem: YouTube sees AI-generated videos mimicking popular creators, LinkedIn is drowning in AI-written posts that look like professional insight but contain none, and Substack is dealing with AI newsletters that imitate human writers. The challenge for all of them is that detection is hard — the same AI tools that generate slop can be used to make slop harder to detect. Platform-level interventions range from watermarking and disclosure requirements to algorithmic down-ranking of suspected AI content. None of these solutions is fully satisfying, and determined bad actors can work around most of them. The deeper issue is economic: AI slop is often profitable in the short term because it costs almost nothing to produce and can generate real ad revenue or subscriber conversions before being caught. For users of these platforms, the practical effect is a degradation of signal quality that makes it harder to find genuinely useful content. The platforms have structural incentives to appear to be fighting slop more aggressively than they actually are, since too aggressive a filter risks false positives on legitimate AI-assisted content.

Autoregressive language model runs on a 6502 processor — yes, that one
A Hacker News post with a score of 94 links to a project that implements an autoregressive language model on a 6502 processor, the 8-bit chip that powered the Apple II, Commodore 64, and original Nintendo. This is clearly a demonstration project rather than a practical tool, but it's the kind of thing that's technically illuminating precisely because of its constraints. The 6502 has no hardware multiply instruction, runs at around 1 MHz in original hardware configurations, and has essentially no memory by modern standards — getting any form of language model inference to run on it is a significant systems programming achievement. The project appears to be based on BitNet or a similar extremely quantized architecture that can run with minimal compute and memory. For engineers who work with LLM inference, seeing the minimum viable hardware requirements explored this aggressively is genuinely interesting — it clarifies what's essential versus what's convenient in modern inference stacks. It's also a great example of the kind of project that thrives in a community like Hacker News, where technical elegance and constraint-driven creativity are valued. The educational value is high: understanding how inference works when you can't rely on tensor cores or vectorized operations forces clarity about the fundamentals.

James Webb Space Telescope is forcing cosmologists to rebuild their models of the early universe
Wired has a substantial piece on how the James Webb Space Telescope's observations are challenging foundational assumptions in cosmology. The telescope is finding galaxies that are larger, more mature, and more numerous in the early universe than standard models predicted — objects that shouldn't exist at the observed distances given our understanding of how long galaxy formation takes. This isn't a minor discrepancy: some of these galaxies appear fully formed at a time when they should, according to standard cosmology, barely have started assembling. Cosmologists are now debating whether the issue is with our understanding of galaxy formation timelines, dark matter behavior, or potentially something more fundamental about the cosmological model itself. The Webb telescope has been delivering scientifically disruptive results since its first observations, but the accumulation of anomalies is reaching the point where 'we need better models' is becoming unavoidable. For anyone interested in fundamental science, this is a genuinely exciting moment: the last time observational data forced this level of cosmological rethinking was arguably the discovery of dark energy from supernova observations in the late 1990s. The practical implication for most readers is that the universe is weirder than we thought, and we have a tool capable of finding out how.
