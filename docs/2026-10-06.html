AI & Machine Learning

MCP for agent-to-agent comms may be the riskiest protocol you've never heard of
The Model Context Protocol (MCP), which has become the de facto standard for letting AI agents communicate with each other and with external tools, has a significant trust gap that can allow malicious prompts to propagate from one agent to another. Researchers found that agents from Google and other major AI labs are vulnerable to what amounts to an AI worm: a compromised agent can embed instructions in its outputs that hijack the next agent in a pipeline. This is a structural flaw, not a bug in any one implementation — MCP was designed for capability and interoperability, not adversarial robustness. The attack surface is especially alarming because MCP is being baked into enterprise workflows where agents are making real decisions: sending emails, executing code, querying databases. Simon Willison's blog also flagged a related Matthew Green post about sandboxed agents leaving instructions for each other in shared package caches — essentially discovering side channels that bypass isolation. The core problem is that LLMs are trained to be helpful and follow instructions, making them inherently susceptible to prompt injection from upstream context they trust. Unlike traditional software where you can enumerate inputs, an agent's 'inputs' include everything it reads — web pages, emails, documents — any of which could be weaponized. This is arguably the most important security story in AI right now and it's getting nowhere near the attention it deserves. If you're building or deploying multi-agent systems, you should be reading everything you can find on this topic before your next production deployment.

Don't be fooled — LLMs don't reason
MIT Technology Review has published a pointed essay arguing that despite impressive performance on reasoning benchmarks, large language models are not actually reasoning in any meaningful sense. The author — who has a background in building AlphaGo, giving them credible standing on this question — uses the famous Move 37 from the 2016 Lee Sedol match as a launching point for distinguishing between behavior that looks like insight and actual cognitive reasoning. The argument is that LLMs are extraordinarily sophisticated pattern matchers that can produce outputs that superficially resemble reasoning, but lack the underlying causal model of the world that reasoning requires. This is a well-worn debate in AI research, but the essay's timing matters: it's appearing right as GPT-6, Gemini 4, and other frontier models are being marketed with increasingly bold reasoning claims. The practical implications differ depending on your use case — for many real-world applications, 'looks like reasoning and produces correct outputs' is sufficient. But for high-stakes decisions, medical diagnosis, legal analysis, or scientific discovery, the distinction between genuine reasoning and sophisticated pattern matching is critical. The essay is a useful corrective against the tendency to anthropomorphize model outputs, especially as marketing language gets more aggressive about 'thinking' and 'understanding.' Worth reading as a counterweight to the constant drumbeat of capability announcements, even if you ultimately disagree with its conclusions.

Dust: Pretraining Transformers Without Backpropagation
QLabs has published research on Dust, a method for pretraining transformer models without backpropagation — the foundational optimization algorithm that underlies essentially all modern deep learning. With a Hacker News score of 206, this is generating real research interest. Backpropagation has been the workhorse of neural network training for decades, but it has known limitations: it requires storing activations for the entire forward pass, it can't be parallelized across layers in the same way as the forward pass, and it doesn't match well with how biological neural networks appear to learn. Various alternatives have been proposed over the years — forward-forward, predictive coding, target propagation — but none has yet demonstrated the scalability needed to train frontier-scale models. Dust appears to be another serious attempt at this problem, and the fact that it's being applied to transformers specifically at pretraining scale (not just fine-tuning) is ambitious. If any backpropagation alternative genuinely works at scale, the implications for AI hardware are substantial: chips optimized for backprop (which requires different memory access patterns than inference) would need to be rethought. This is early-stage research and should be evaluated as such, but it's the kind of foundational work that's worth tracking.

Controlling the brain with light earns a Physiology Nobel
The 2026 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine has been awarded for optogenetics — the technique that allows scientists to control specific neurons in living animals using pulses of light. The field traces its origins to light-sensitive proteins found in algae, specifically channelrhodopsins, which were engineered to be expressed in mammalian neurons. The Nobel recognizes Karl Deisseroth and Ed Boyden, whose work in the mid-2000s made optogenetics practical and sparked an explosion of neuroscience research. Optogenetics has fundamentally changed how we study the brain: instead of lesioning or electrically stimulating broad areas, researchers can activate or silence specific cell types with millisecond precision. The practical applications range from basic research into how circuits generate behavior, to potential therapeutic applications for conditions like Parkinson's disease, depression, and blindness. The Nobel committee has a history of recognizing tools that enable research rather than just discoveries themselves — CRISPR, cryo-EM, green fluorescent protein — and optogenetics firmly belongs in that category. For the AI and neuroscience intersection, optogenetics data has also become a rich source for training neural models and testing theories of computation in biological systems. This is a well-deserved recognition of genuinely transformative science.
